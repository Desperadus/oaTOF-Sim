Why Orthogonal Acceleration Exists

A time-of-flight analyser measures one thing: how long an ion takes to travel a fixed path after being released at a known instant. That demands a pulsed start. Everything that feeds it — electrospray, an ion funnel, a collision cell — delivers a continuous beam.

Orthogonal acceleration resolves the mismatch by accelerating a slab of the beam at right angles to its own motion. The energy given along the flight axis is thousands of electronvolts; the few electronvolts the ion already had along the beam axis are untouched and survive the entire flight. That surviving drift is why the path is a V and why the detector sits well to one side of the pusher.

Time of Flight

After acceleration through a potential U an ion of mass m and charge Q has

½mv² = QU  →  v = √(2QU/m)  →  t = L / v = L · √(m / 2QU)

so flight time goes as √(m/z). The charge cancels between the energy gained and the mass accelerated, which is why this is a mass-to-charge measurement. Every part of the flight scales the same way — the acceleration, the drift and the time spent inside the mirror — so the whole mass scale is fixed by one constant, plus an offset for delays that are not flight at all.

Duty Cycle: The Price of Orthogonality

Between pushes the beam keeps flowing. If the pusher aperture is Lp long and the beam crosses it at vx, then in a push period T the beam advances vxT and only the last Lp of it is still inside when the pusher fires:

duty cycle = min(1, Lp / (vx T))     with   vx = √(2QUbeam/m)

Because vx falls as 1/√(m/z), heavy ions have a better duty cycle than light ones. This is real mass discrimination built into the geometry, and it is the reason an orthogonal instrument's sensitivity is not flat across the mass range. This simulation does not apply that formula — it streams ions through the pusher and counts whatever is inside at the moment of the shot.

What Limits Resolution

Resolution is R = t / 2Δt. Four things widen the peak, and they are measured separately in the Budget tab:

  • Turn-around time. An ion drifting backwards must be stopped before it can leave. It rejoins the trajectory of its forward-moving twin delayed by Δt = 2mvz/(QE), where vz is thermal. Only a stronger pusher field or a colder beam reduces it, and in a well-tuned instrument it is what sets the ceiling.
  • Position in the pusher. The slab has thickness, so ions start at different potentials and leave with different energies. This is the largest term before the mirror deals with it, and the smallest after.
  • Beam height. Ions off the axis see a slightly different mirror potential and turn around at a slightly different depth.
  • Beam energy spread. Spread in the drift velocity, which changes where an ion lands on the detector rather than when.

The Two-Stage Ion Mirror

An ion with more energy penetrates further into a retarding field, so it travels a longer path and takes longer to come back. Choose the field correctly and that extra path exactly cancels the extra speed. One uniform stage cancels the energy dependence to first order; two stages with independently chosen fields cancel it to second order, and that is the difference between a resolution of a few hundred and a resolution of tens of thousands.

Nothing in this simulation is told where the second-order tune is. The auto-tune searches both mirror voltages for the pair that flattens arrival time across the beam, and the focus curve collapses by four orders of magnitude when it finds it.

A Gridless Mirror Is Not A Linear Ramp

Putting grids in a mirror would make the field textbook-uniform and scatter a large fraction of the ions on every pass, so real mirrors are gridless. The price is that the potential inside is no longer the potential on the plates. Laplace's equation is solved here inside the mirror aperture with the plate potentials on the wall:

∇²Φ = 0   inside,   Φ = plate ramp on the wall

Two consequences appear on their own. The field leaks out through the entrance, decaying over roughly the aperture half-height, which is the entrance lens. And the potential varies across the aperture, so an ion off the axis turns around at a different depth and arrives at a different time.

That second effect is worth watching, because it is the largest term in the default budget. Thermal motion across the flight axis carries an ion off centre, and the time it costs grows roughly as the square of how far it strays — so the peak ends up with a sharp core and long tails rather than a Gaussian shape. Switch the mirror to the ideal ramp and the beam-height term falls to exactly zero, because a ramp with no transverse field cannot do this at all.

An honest limitation. The mirror modelled here has a plain linear potential ramp along its wall. Real gridless mirrors are stacks of many electrodes whose voltages are tuned individually to flatten the field across the aperture, precisely to suppress this term. The transverse behaviour you see is therefore a fair consequence of a simple two-stage gridless mirror, and worse than a well-engineered one.

Trapped Ion Mobility

In a TIMS tunnel the drift gas pushes ions forward while an electric field gradient holds them back. An ion parks where the two balance:

K · E = vgas  →  Eelution = vgas / K

Lowering the field releases ions in order of the field they need. Large, low-mobility ions need the highest field and therefore elute first — the opposite order to a drift tube. The mobility at tunnel conditions follows from the definition of reduced mobility, K = K₀·(P₀/P)·(T/T₀); the collision cross-section follows from mobility through the Mason–Schamp relation, which is not computed here.

Mobility separates what mass cannot: two ions of identical m/z and different shape are one peak in the spectrum and two spots on the mobility map. Note what TIMS does not fix by itself — the pusher duty cycle is set by the geometry and the push rate, and stays exactly where it was unless the release is short enough and synchronised to the shot.

How The Flight Is Integrated

The pusher, the accelerator and the drift each have a field that is constant across the region, so a step taken inside one of them is the exact solution however long it is; steps are cut short so they land precisely on every region boundary. Averaging two different accelerations across a field discontinuity is the single largest error available in a simulation like this, and avoiding it is what makes the turn-around time come out equal to 2mvz/(QE) to machine precision. Only inside the mirror, where the solved field genuinely varies from point to point, is velocity Verlet doing real work.
